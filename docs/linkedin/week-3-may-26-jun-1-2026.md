AI generates employer narratives from a structured set of twelve source categories grouped into five families based on who controls the surface.

Employer-owned (4): careers site, company newsroom and blog, functional team content (engineering blog, fleet-safety blog, design blog), and content authored by named leaders on platforms outside the company domain.

Peer-reviewed (3): Glassdoor, generalist platforms (Comparably, Indeed, Kununu), and sector-specific platforms (Built In, Vault, Wall Street Oasis).

Specialist (3): compensation platforms (Levels.fyi, RepVue), persona-specific communities (Blind, Gainsight Community, Pavilion, SHRM communities), and public professional forums (Reddit, Hacker News).

Independent (1): named-author content on platforms the author controls. Substack newsletters, podcast transcripts, conference talks, named-author Medium.

Authoritative (1): editorial press. Trade publications, regional business journals, the major business desks.

The taxonomy is sector-agnostic. A regional bank, a specialty retailer, a national logistics company, and a late-stage SaaS firm all have presence and absence across the same twelve categories. Only the platform names within each category shift.

Knowing the map is the first move. Most of the conversation about "what AI says about us" lives inside it.

Glassdoor presence is binary: either a company has a Glassdoor page or it doesn't. The harder question, which actually predicts what AI cites when a candidate asks about a company, is what kind of presence that page has.

Same surface, four observable depth tiers in the scored corpus:

Anchored: Glassdoor citations recur across many candidate-intent queries; AI is pulling from multiple distinct reviews authored by different employees; the most-recent cited review is from the last few months.

Present: citations show up but inconsistently across queries; the cited material has limited voice diversity; recency is mixed.

Thin: AI is citing the page, but the citation traces back to one or two reviews, often older, no recent material entering the synthesis.

Absent: Glassdoor exists but isn't being cited by AI in this corpus.

Three components determine the tier: density of distinct cited material, voice diversity (how many named humans authored what AI is pulling from), and recency.

A regional bank can be Anchored on Glassdoor for branch-network roles and Thin for treasury-management roles in the same scan. Same surface. Two different depth reads. Two materially different candidate experiences.

The question isn't whether a company is on Glassdoor. The question is what AI is reading from it.

Senior customer success manager roles are a large hiring line at mid-market and growth-stage software companies, where 30 to 80 net new hires per year is common. The same role pattern shows up in financial services post-sale teams and in professional services firms, just under different titles.

The amount of role-specific narrative AI generates for those same companies, when a candidate asks "is [company] a good place to grow a customer success career," runs thin in the scored corpus. The thin surfaces are career-path content on the company-owned domain, named customer success leader voices, and customer success learning content. When these appear in candidate-intent answers at all, they appear in one or two of every dozen queries on the role.

The pattern lives inside the twelve-category citation taxonomy from Tuesday's post. Most of what AI cites for senior customer success queries comes from Specialist surfaces (Gainsight Community, Customer Success Network, ChurnZero podcast appearances). Employer-owned surfaces, where companies have the most direct control over what AI reads, typically carry the least.

Twelve categories. The throughput asymmetry shows up in different ones depending on the role.
